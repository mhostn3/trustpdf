Someone has sent you a contract, a lease or a consent form to sign. Printing it, signing it with a pen, scanning it and emailing it back works, but it takes a printer, a scanner and twenty minutes. Signing services work too, but they upload the document and often want an account. Here is how to sign a PDF on your own computer or phone, free, in about a minute.

How to sign a PDF

  1. Open Sign PDF and drop the document into the box.
  2. Create your signature in one of three ways:
    • Draw: sign with the mouse, a touchpad, or your finger on a phone or tablet.
    • Type: type your name and pick a script or handwritten style.
    • Upload image: use a photo or scan of your signature on white paper; tick the option to make the white background transparent.
  3. Go to the right page and drag the signature onto the signature line. Adjust its width.
  4. Optionally tick Add today's date under the signature, or Put it on every page for initials.
  5. Click Sign PDF, then Save Signed PDF, and send the saved file back.

The document and your signature stay on your device the whole time. Nothing is uploaded and nothing is stored: when you close the page, the signature is gone.

If the document has form fields

Many contracts and applications have fields for your name, address and date. Fill those in first with Fill PDF Form, and tick Flatten the form so your answers become part of the page. Then add your signature as the last step. See how to fill in a PDF form without uploading it.

Is it legally valid?

Placing an image of your signature on a document creates what the law calls a simple electronic signature. In the EU, the eIDAS regulation says an electronic signature cannot be refused as evidence just because it is electronic, and in the US the ESIGN Act and UETA give electronic signatures the same standing as handwritten ones for most transactions. For everyday contracts, rental agreements, HR forms and consent forms, this is usually all that is needed.

Some documents need more: certain property transfers, court documents, wills and notarial deeds, or anything where the other party asks for a "qualified" or "certified" digital signature. Those use a cryptographic certificate issued by a trust service provider, which this kind of signature is not. When in doubt, ask the person who sent you the document what they accept. This is general information, not legal advice.

Tips

  • Sign last. Adding pages, merging or filling forms after signing can make it look as if the document changed after you signed it.
  • If you want to stop casual editing of the signed file, protect it with an owner password and editing switched off.
  • Keep a copy of exactly what you signed.
