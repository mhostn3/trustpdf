Search for "merge PDF" or "PDF to Word" and almost every result works the same way: you upload your document, a server processes it, and you download the result. For a restaurant menu, that is fine. For a passport scan, a tax return, a payslip or a signed contract, it is worth knowing what actually happens.

What happens when you upload a PDF

  1. Your file travels over the internet to the service's servers, which may be in another country and run by a cloud provider you have never heard of.
  2. It is stored on a disk there while it is processed, and the result is stored too, so you can download it.
  3. The service promises to delete both after some time, often one or a few hours. Some keep them longer if you have an account.

Reputable services do delete files and protect their servers well. But you have no way to check: the deletion happens on a computer you cannot see. Copies can survive in backups, logs, caches or error reports, and a server that holds thousands of people's documents is an attractive target for attackers.

The real risks

  • Data breaches. Any server that stores uploaded files can be breached, and a PDF service sees a steady stream of IDs, contracts and financial records.
  • Confidentiality obligations. Lawyers, accountants, doctors and HR staff are often not allowed to hand client data to a third party without an agreement. Under GDPR, uploading someone's personal data to a random website can itself be a breach.
  • Unclear terms. Free services have to pay for their servers somehow. Read the terms: some reserve the right to analyse documents, or are owned by companies whose business is data.
  • Fake tools. Malicious "converter" websites exist to collect documents or to serve malware with the result.

The alternative: tools that run in your browser

Modern browsers are powerful enough to process PDFs themselves. A browser-based tool sends the program to you instead of asking you to send the file to it. Your document is read from your disk into the web page, processed by JavaScript on your own computer or phone, and saved back to your disk. It never crosses the internet, so there is nothing to store, leak or delete.

That is how every TrustPDF tool works: merging, compressing, converting to Word, signing, redacting, OCR and the rest. The code is open source, so anyone can check what it does.

How to tell whether a tool uploads your file

  • Watch for an upload. A progress bar that says "uploading", a queue, or a wait for "the server" means the file left your device.
  • Go offline. Open the tool, disconnect from Wi-Fi, then use it. A browser-based tool keeps working; an upload-based one fails.
  • Look at the Network tab of your browser's developer tools while you process a file. An upload shows up as a large outgoing request.

The guide How to check that a PDF tool doesn't upload your files walks through all three tests.

A short checklist before you use any PDF website

  • Does it say clearly whether files are uploaded? Vague wording such as "secure processing" usually means they are.
  • Does it still work offline after the page has loaded?
  • Is the source code public?
  • Does it need an account or an email address for a simple task? Then it is collecting something.
  • Would you be comfortable emailing this document to a stranger? If not, do not upload it.
